AI Literacy & Governance Awareness Resource

AI Across Health Professions Training: Exposure, Evidence & Safe-Use Questions

A representative map of where students, faculty, and trainees may encounter AI across affiliate clinical sites, classrooms, research workflows, and general-purpose tools.

AI governance at a health sciences center should not start with a vendor list. It should start with an exposure map: which students, faculty, and trainees encounter which AI tools, in which settings, under which privacy, documentation, clinical, and affiliate-site rules.

AI exposure is expanding faster than institutional guidance. This resource helps faculty, trainees, and staff recognize where AI is already present so they can ask the right governance, privacy, and supervision questions before using it.

Tool examples are included to illustrate AI exposure and governance needs. Inclusion does not imply endorsement, procurement recommendation, clinical approval, or permission for use with protected data.

How to use this resource
This page is an educational awareness resource. It is not an approved-tool list, procurement recommendation, clinical-use policy, or legal or compliance determination. Faculty, trainees, and staff should follow institutional policies, affiliate-site policies, HIPAA requirements, IRB requirements, and program-specific guidance before using AI tools with patient, student, research, or institutional data.
AI exposure now spans every health profession. Because AI tools are evolving quickly across clinical, educational, and research settings, programs across the health sciences center benefit from a shared cross-school reference that helps faculty, trainees, and staff understand where AI exposure is occurring and what questions to ask.
For you

What this means for you

  • Faculty: Do not recommend AI tools for clinical, educational, or research workflows unless privacy, data security, and institutional rules are clear.
  • Trainees: Do not enter PHI, student data, unpublished research data, or affiliate-site clinical information into consumer AI tools.
  • Staff: Be careful with AI-generated emails, reports, spreadsheets, student records, admissions data, HR data, and financial data.
  • Program directors: Ask affiliate sites what AI tools students encounter during rotations and what site-specific rules apply to students from external programs.
  • IT and compliance: Build a visible approved-use and BAA inventory accessible to all schools.
Overview

How AI enters the health sciences center

Schools

Medicine, Nursing, Allied Health, Dentistry, Public Health, and Graduate Studies.

AI types encountered

Clinical AI, educational AI, research AI, administrative AI, and consumer/general-purpose AI.

Governance layer

Privacy, BAAs, documentation, affiliate policy, human review, and approved-use inventory.

Local

AI already deployed at affiliate sites

Trainees rotate through LCMC Health, Ochsner Health, OLOL, and other affiliate sites. Each site governs its own AI tools. Home programs need to understand which tools trainees encounter and which policy applies.

LCMC Health: Nabla

Systemwide ambient AI implementation integrated with Epic. Trainees may encounter AI-generated documentation in clinical workflows.

Source: PR Newswire, Dec 3 2025: LCMC Health selects Nabla

Ochsner Health: DeepScribe

Ambient AI documentation across 4,700+ clinicians. Trainees may work inside a site-governed AI documentation environment.

Source: Ochsner Health news release, Jul 30 2024

Epic AI

AI charting, note drafting, order queueing, patient-message drafting, handoff summaries, billing support, and scheduling-related workflows embedded in the EHR.

Source: Epic.com: Art for Clinicians

OLOL and other affiliates

Policies and tool inventories may differ by site. Public confirmation should be obtained before describing site-specific AI tools.

Disciplines

Representative AI exposure by discipline

Medicine
OpenEvidence, UpToDate Expert AI, ambient scribes, Epic AI functions, and residency application screening tools such as Thalamus/Cortex.
Nursing
GoodNurse, Shadow Health, Epic nursing workflows, virtual nursing and ambient monitoring, fall risk, sepsis alerts, deterioration scores, medication and infusion systems.
Allied Health
PT and OT documentation AI, motion and gait analytics, remote therapeutic monitoring, respiratory therapy ICU tools, SLP/audiology documentation and speech analytics, and radiologic technology AI triage.
Dentistry
Dental radiology AI, caries detection, bone-loss measurement, treatment planning, patient communication overlays, and coding or billing tools.
Public Health
Disease surveillance, population analytics, public health messaging, emergency preparedness, and AI-assisted research and grants workflows.
Graduate Studies and Research
Literature synthesis, scientific writing, coding, data analysis, biomedical research AI, grant intelligence, and research integrity concerns.
General

General-purpose AI tools students and faculty actually use

The most common AI tools are consumer and general-purpose: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Grok, Perplexity, and NotebookLM. Free or personal accounts should not be used with PHI, student records, unpublished research data, HR data, financial data, or other protected institutional information.

Before use

Five questions to ask before using any AI tool

  1. Is this tool institutionally approved?
  2. Does it handle PHI or other sensitive institutional data?
  3. Is human review required before the output is used?
  4. Which affiliate policy applies?
  5. Where can you report concerns?
Evidence

Evidence tiers used on this page

FDA-cleared

AI-enabled medical devices authorized for clinical use by the FDA.

Peer-reviewed

Tools or categories with published clinical or educational outcome data.

Professional guidance

Categories addressed by professional bodies such as ANA, APTA, ASHA, AMA, or AAMC.

Emerging

Tools in active deployment without full peer-reviewed validation or standardized institutional guidance.

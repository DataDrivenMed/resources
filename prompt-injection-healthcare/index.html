Prompt injection in healthcare

An AI can sometimes treat instructions hidden in a document, webpage, email, image, or clinical record as though you gave them. When that AI can also reach sensitive data or take actions, a distorted answer can become disclosure, record alteration, operational disruption, or patient-safety harm.

01

The instruction arrives inside content.

Indirect prompt injection may be concealed in material the AI is asked to read, summarize, retrieve, classify, or act upon. The user may never see or type it.

02

Content and instructions share the context.

The AI receives system rules, the user task, retrieved information, and tool results together. Applications can prioritize trusted instructions, but no perfect instruction-data boundary currently exists.

03

The AI uses what it can reach.

A standalone chatbot may only change its answer. A connected assistant may also search institutional repositories, use an authenticated session, or call software tools.

04

The manipulated behavior becomes harm.

The AI may omit a warning, retrieve unrelated information, expose PHI or institutional IP, write misleading text, send a message, alter a workflow, or invoke a dangerous tool.

What prompt injection can actually do

From a wrong answer to a system-level event.

Prompt injection does not create universal access. It manipulates the AI into misusing access that already exists.

🔑

Permissions form the ceiling of harm.
The AI can generally reach only the records, folders, applications, credentials, tools, and actions made available by the surrounding system.

🧾

Manipulated clinical output

Omit, distort, reprioritize, or fabricate information while appearing to complete the requested task.

Requires: untrusted content and reliance on the generated answer.
🔓

PHI or institutional IP exposure

Retrieve and reveal reachable patient data, unpublished research, proprietary methods, contracts, strategy, credentials, or operational knowledge.

Requires: read access plus a visible output or external channel.
✉️

Unauthorized communication

Send email, share files, upload documents, post content, or disclose information to the wrong destination.

Requires: messaging, browser, drive, or upload tools.
✍️

Record and workflow alteration

Insert misleading text, modify fields, change status, delete information, or alter schedules and queues.

Requires: write access to the EHR, database, or workflow tool.
🪪

Misuse of authenticated authority

Act through the user's account, API token, connector, or non-human service identity.

Requires: delegated credentials and weak action-level approval.
♻️

Persistent manipulation

Remain in a repository, email archive, retrieval corpus, application record, or memory and influence later tasks.

Requires: storage and repeated retrieval, sometimes with write access.
💊

Patient-safety harm

Change triage, medication advice, documentation, evidence synthesis, or warnings that influence care.

Requires: downstream clinical use without adequate verification.
⏱️

Operational or financial disruption

Trigger loops, repeated searches, excessive tool calls, delays, or avoidable token and cloud costs.

Requires: automation or metered services without effective limits.
⌨️

Technical system compromise

In a dangerously designed agent, attacker-influenced text may reach code, shell, or administrative tools.

Requires: powerful execution tools plus unsafe integration.
Prompt injection is not just a bad prompt. It is a trust, permissions, and system-design problem.

Clarifying the mechanism

Can it get “inside the data”?

A malicious instruction can be stored inside a document, record, email, knowledge base, vector database, or memory and manipulate the AI when retrieved later. This is persistent hostile content, not the same as conventional malware automatically infecting every file. Further spread or alteration requires write access and additional system capabilities.

Direct injection

The manipulating instruction is entered directly into the conversation.

Indirect injection

The instruction is carried by a PDF, webpage, message, image, scan, tool result, or retrieved record.

Healthcare pathways

Where the instruction can enter.

💬

Patient portal message

Hidden content attempts to change a summary or redirect the assistant toward unrelated chart information.

📠

Scanned outside record

Low-contrast text influences extraction, transfer documentation, or clinical prioritization.

🌐

Medical webpage

A browsing assistant is redirected toward confidential files or an outbound request.

🖼️

Clinical image

Embedded visual content changes how a vision-language model describes or interprets an image.

📚

Institutional retrieval corpus

A compromised policy or guideline is repeatedly retrieved and skews later answers.

🤖

Tool-using agent

Manipulated content influences an agent that can email, upload, schedule, document, or modify records.

A five-question safety check

PAUSE before AI reaches data or actions.

PAUSE

P · Permissions

What records, applications, credentials, folders, tools, or actions can the AI access?

A · Approved tool and account

Is this exact product, account type, feature, and workflow approved here?

U · Untrusted content

Is the AI reading material an outside party or external system could manipulate?

S · Sensitive or strategic information

Does the task involve PHI, credentials, research, plans, contracts, or proprietary knowledge?

E · External action and exposure

Can the AI send, upload, retain, disclose, modify, publish, order, or schedule anything?

PAUSE is an educational framework created for this resource. It is not an externally validated clinical or regulatory standard.

For clinicians, trainees, faculty, and researchers

What to do before and during use.

  • Use only institutionally approved tools for institutional or clinical information.
  • Confirm the exact product, account, feature, permitted data, PHI conditions, and clinical site.
  • Do not assume a paid or business account is approved for PHI.
  • Grant the smallest necessary permissions and avoid broad, undefined agent requests.
  • Verify the information and destination before any upload, message, record change, order, or external action.
  • Independently verify clinical content before patient-care or medical-record use.

Stop when the AI...

  • unexpectedly changes the task;
  • asks for credentials or broader access;
  • proposes sending information elsewhere;
  • retrieves unrelated confidential material;
  • acts before showing the exact data and destination.
Do not test suspected malicious content in a production clinical environment.

Executive governance chapter

For CMIOs, CIOs, CISOs, privacy officers, and AI governance leaders.

Prompt injection is one risk among several. Data retention, model improvement, feedback, excessive agency, and institutional IP dependency require separate controls and contractual review.

Institutional AI sovereignty

The practical ability to govern institutional data, models, workflows, permissions, evaluation assets, costs, and operational knowledge, including the ability to audit, export, change providers, and maintain continuity.

What healthcare leaders should ask Click to collapse or expand
  • Is this a consumer, institutionally managed, or contracted enterprise product?
  • Who owns prompts, outputs, workflow configurations, evaluation datasets, and institutional knowledge?
  • What is retained, for how long, and under which configuration?
  • What changes when a user submits feedback?
  • What does zero data retention cover, and what operational records remain?
  • Can the institution audit what the AI accessed and every action it took?
  • Can prompts, workflows, evaluations, and institutional knowledge be exported?
  • Can the workflow switch models without being rebuilt?
  • Is a BAA available and applicable to this exact product and workflow?
  • Who reviews changes in models, features, pricing, and data handling?

How current guidance frames the problem

Recurring principles, without personality profiles.

Model safety and reliability Click to expand

Capable systems remain imperfect. High-consequence use requires meaningful supervision, controlled deployment, independent verification, and explicit limits on authority.

Agent and application security Click to expand

The highest-risk pattern combines private information, untrusted content, and a channel that can communicate or act outside the task. Permission and action boundaries matter as much as model behavior.

Enterprise governance and institutional control Click to expand

Organizations should know what data, infrastructure, workflow configurations, evaluation assets, and institutional knowledge they control. Portability, pricing, auditability, and contractual protection are governance questions.

Standards and healthcare leadership Click to expand

Prompt injection should be addressed through lifecycle governance, risk ownership, identity, approved use, monitoring, incident response, patient safety, downtime planning, and recurring reassessment rather than a single filter.

When something looks wrong

Stop, contain, preserve, report.

1

Stop

End the AI task and do not authorize further actions.

2

Contain

Disconnect or revoke unnecessary tool access when permitted and safe.

3

Preserve

Capture prompts, outputs, timestamps, destinations, and affected records according to policy.

4

Report

Use the approved IT, privacy, security, compliance, clinical-informatics, or patient-safety process.

Local policy matters

Ask the institution and the clinical site.

Public guidance is incomplete and can change. This review did not locate a current comprehensive approved-product list for LSU Health New Orleans, LCMC Health, Ochsner Health, or Our Lady of the Lake Health. That does not mean internal guidance is absent.

Obtain a current update from local IT, information security, privacy, compliance, clinical informatics, or AI governance.
  • Which tools, accounts, and features are approved?
  • May any approved product receive PHI, and under what agreement?
  • Which data types are prohibited?
  • May AI connect to email, drives, calendars, browsers, or the EHR?
  • Are browser or computer-use agents permitted?
  • Are AI-generated notes permitted in the medical record?
  • What retention, model-improvement, and feedback rules apply?
  • How should suspicious behavior be reported?
  • Does approval at one clinical site extend to another?

Content can become instructions. Access determines the blast radius. Use PAUSE.
